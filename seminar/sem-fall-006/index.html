A CompSci Talks seminar on December 6, 2025 at 1:00 PM.

Abstract

# Lecture Summary: Ontological Approaches and Challenges in KRR **Session Topic:** Moving from data storage to machine understanding through Knowledge Representation, Reasoning, and Ontologies. **Main Objective:** To enable machines to understand the relationships between concepts (semantics) rather than just treating data as disconnected text strings. --- ## 1. Introduction to Knowledge Representation and Reasoning (KRR) The core challenge in AI is enabling machines to go beyond storing and retrieving data to a stage of **understanding**. * **The Human vs. Machine Gap:** Humans can infer that "Paracetamol" is a drug used for "Fever" (a symptom). A computer sees these only as text strings unless the relationship is explicitly defined. * **Definition of KRR:** A field of Artificial Intelligence dedicated to representing information about the world in a form that a computer system can utilize to solve complex tasks, such as logical inference and decision-making. ## 2. Defining "Knowledge" The lecture defines knowledge through the **Tripartite Analysis (Plato's Definition)**: * **Knowledge** is a justified subset of all true beliefs. * It requires three components: **Truth**, **Belief**, and **Justification** (evidence). ## 3. Approaches to Knowledge Representation Knowledge representation is generally divided into two main categories: ### A. Logical-Based Approaches * **First-Order Logic (FOL):** Uses propositional and predicate logic. * **Rule-Based Systems:** Utilizes "If-Then" statements to represent logic. ### B. Non-Logical-Based Approaches * **Semantic Networks:** Graphical representations of knowledge using nodes (entities/concepts) and edges (relationships). * **Frames:** Similar to data structure records; they use "slots" or attributes to describe objects, events, or typical situations. --- ## 4. Ontology: The Foundation of Understanding In computer science, an ontology is the "constitution" of a domain, defined by experts to eliminate ambiguity. * **Gruber�s Definition:** An explicit, formal specification of a shared conceptualization. * **Key Characteristics:** 1. **Conceptualization:** An abstract model of a domain. 2. **Explicit:** Concepts must be defined rigidly to avoid misinterpretation. 3. **Formal:** Must be machine-readable and interpretable. * **Building Blocks:** * **Classes:** Concepts or categories (e.g., "Person", "Drug"). * **Relations/Properties:** How classes connect (e.g., "treats", "is-a"). * **Instances/Individuals:** Specific members of a class (e.g., "Tom Hanks" is an instance of "Actor"). --- ## 5. Ontological Languages and Standards (W3C) ### RDF (Resource Description Framework) * Used to represent simple facts. * **The Triple:** The basic unit of RDF consisting of **Subject � Predicate � Object**. * **Identification:** Uses IRIs (Internationalized Resource Identifiers) to uniquely identify resources. * **Literals:** Plain data values like numbers or dates. * **Serialization:** Discussed **Turtle (Terse RDF Triple Language)** as a human-readable format that uses prefixes to simplify IRIs. ### RDFS (RDF Schema) * Extends RDF to allow for the definition of hierarchies and categories. * **T-Box (Terminological Knowledge):** Defines the schema (Classes, subClassOf, subPropertyOf, Domain, and Range). * **A-Box (Assertion Knowledge):** States facts about individuals (Instances). ### OWL (Web Ontology Language) * Necessary because RDFS is limited (it cannot represent negation, disjointness, or complex constraints). * Based on **Description Logic (DL)**. * **OWL 2 DL:** The most expressive yet decidable version. * **Key Concepts:** * **Open World Assumption (OWA):** If a fact is not stated, it is assumed to be "unknown" rather than "false." * **Unique Name Assumption (UNA):** OWL does *not* assume different names refer to different things; one must explicitly state if individuals are the same or different (e.g., "Tom Hanks" vs "Thomas Hanks"). --- ## 6. Challenges in Knowledge Representation The speaker highlighted several "bottlenecks" in the field: 1. **The Expressivity-Efficiency Trade-off:** The more complex the logic (expressivity), the harder and slower it is for a machine to process (reasoning). 2. **Knowledge Acquisition Bottleneck:** * **Tacit Knowledge:** Expert knowledge based on experience that is difficult to articulate or formalize. * **Ambiguity:** Natural language is often vague. * **Inconsistency:** Different experts or data sources may provide conflicting information. 3. **Scalability:** Difficulty in maintaining ontologies as domains grow rapidly. --- ## 7. Future Directions: Neuro-symbolic AI The lecture concludes with the potential of **Hybrid Systems**: * **Symbolic AI (Ontologies):** Excellent at abstraction and logic but poor at learning from raw data. * **Sub-symbolic AI (Deep Learning/LLMs):** Excellent at learning patterns but prone to "hallucinations" and lack of logical grounding. * **Goal:** Combining these two to create systems that can learn from data while adhering to a logical, verifiable knowledge base (e.g., using LLMs to extract triples for a Knowledge Graph, which then verifies the LLM's output).
